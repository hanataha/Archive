## Grok

The first cry was ordinary.

In the deep night of the ninth month, when the mountain wind that always swept the outer courtyards of the Azure Dragon Pavilion finally fell still, a newborn’s voice rose once, thin and sharp, then settled into the wet, indignant breathing of a healthy infant. Servants moved with practiced quiet. Midwives wiped blood and vernix. The clan physician checked the pulse at the tiny wrist, nodded once, and reported that the qi was steady, the bones sound, the lungs clear.

They named him Eon.

He was the first son of the main line of the Baek clan of Mount Cheongryong. His father was Baek Mun-gyeok, the current clan head, a man whose fist had once shattered the outer formation of a rival sect’s mountain gate in a single afternoon. His mother was Lady Seo Hye-rin of the southern Seo clan, brought north in marriage fifteen years earlier; her internal energy was not as explosive as her husband’s, but her control was finer, and the physicians said the child had inherited the clarity of her meridians.

Eon, lying in the lacquered cradle with its carved cloud-and-dragon motif, opened his eyes on the second day and looked at the ceiling beams without focus. The adults around him saw only a baby. Inside the small body, something else was already awake.

He had done this before. Not in this exact world, not under this exact name, but the sensation of soft limbs, the sudden helplessness of a neck that could not yet hold its own head, the way sound arrived muffled and too large—he recognized the pattern. Another Murim world. Another major clan. Another body that would have to be grown from nothing while the mind behind the eyes remembered older skies.

He decided, in the wordless way of a mind that had already chosen many times, that he would not stand out.

The decision was not dramatic. There was no oath sworn to the heavens, no dramatic internal monologue that the nurses could have overheard. He simply let his hands stay clumsy when the physician tested his grip. He cried when he was hungry and quieted when he was fed. He did not try to focus his eyes too sharply on the faces that leaned over the cradle. When the clan’s old sword master visited on the seventh day to offer a blessing, Eon turned his face into the blanket and made a small, ordinary fuss until the man chuckled and left.

The first year passed in the slow rhythm of a well-guarded inner courtyard.

Lady Seo spent most of her mornings in the nursery wing. She was not an overly demonstrative woman; her affection showed in the way she checked the temperature of the bath water herself, in the way she corrected the wet nurse’s posture when the woman held the child too tightly, and in the quiet conversations she held with her son while she combed her own hair by the window. She spoke of the mountain, of the way the mist moved between the peaks in autumn, of the sound the training bells made at dawn. Eon listened. Sometimes he made the soft, unformed sounds expected of an infant. Sometimes he simply watched the way light moved across the carved wooden screen.

His father visited less often. Baek Mun-gyeok was busy. The Azure Dragon Pavilion held three branch families, two allied minor clans, and a network of martial halls that trained outer disciples. There were border disputes with the Black Tiger Sect to the west and careful, smiling negotiations with the White Phoenix Pavilion to the east. When the clan head did appear, he would stand at the foot of the cradle with his hands clasped behind his back and look at his son for a long moment. Then he would nod, once, and leave. The servants said the young master had his father’s eyes. Eon, when he was alone, practiced making those eyes unfocused.

By the time he could sit without support, the clan had already begun to measure him in the small, ordinary ways they measured every child of the main line. Could he grasp a wooden training sword without dropping it? Could he stay quiet during the morning incense? Did his cries disturb the meditation hall? He met every measurement at the lower edge of acceptable. The wooden sword slipped from his fingers twice out of three times. He stayed quiet, but only because he had already learned that the meditation hall’s outer corridor had a loose floorboard that creaked if you shifted your weight a certain way; he simply did not shift. When he cried, it was for the usual reasons—hunger, a wet cloth, the cold that came through the paper windows on winter nights—and he stopped when those reasons were removed.

The second year brought walking.

He took his first steps in the spring courtyard while his mother sat under the plum tree and watched. The steps were uneven. He fell once, sat for a moment with a puzzled expression, then pushed himself up again and continued. The wet nurse clapped. Lady Seo smiled, small and private. Later that afternoon, when no one was looking, Eon walked the length of the veranda again, this time placing each foot with the same deliberate uncertainty. The floorboards did not creak. He made sure of it.

Language arrived the way it always did: first the names of the people who fed him, then the names of the places he was allowed to go, then the small prohibitions. Do not touch the ancestor tablets. Do not run in the inner hall. Do not stare at the elders when they pass. He obeyed. He also learned, without anyone teaching him, which servants would look the other way if a child wandered a little farther than he was supposed to, and which ones would report it immediately. He stayed within the first category’s tolerance and never tested the second.

At three, he was given a proper name plate and a small set of training clothes. The clothes were cut from the same indigo cloth used for the outer disciples, only smaller. He put them on without complaint and stood where the junior instructor told him to stand. The instructor was a woman in her late twenties, Baek So-yeon of a side branch, known for her patience with children and her short temper with anyone who wasted her time. She taught the basic horse stance, the first three movements of the clan’s foundational fist form, and the correct way to breathe so that the breath did not catch in the throat.

Eon learned all of it. He also learned how to make the stance look a little unstable at the end of the count, how to let his fist drift half a finger’s width off the line, how to breathe just loudly enough that So-yeon would correct him and move on. She wrote in her monthly report that the young master showed ordinary aptitude and ordinary diligence. The report was filed. No one called him a prodigy. No one whispered that the main line’s first son was unusually sharp. That was exactly what he wanted.

The clan’s children trained together in the outer practice yard three mornings a week. There were cousins from the branch families, a few children of trusted retainers, and the daughter of the clan’s chief physician. They were loud. They competed over who could hold the stance longest, who could recite the clan precepts without stumbling, who had been allowed to watch the senior disciples spar the day before. Eon stayed near the middle of the group. When they raced, he finished neither first nor last. When they argued about whose father was stronger, he listened and did not offer an opinion. Once, a boy two years older tried to push him during a game of tag. Eon let himself stumble, sat down, and looked up with the blank, slightly confused expression of a child who had not expected the shove. The older boy was scolded by the instructor. Eon did not mention it again.

At night, in the small room that had been prepared for him once he outgrew the cradle, he lay on his back and listened to the mountain. The wind moved differently here than in other places he had lived. It carried the scent of pine resin and the distant mineral smell of the hot springs that fed the clan’s bathhouses. Somewhere below the main compound, the night watch changed shifts; their boots made a soft, regular sound on the stone paths. He counted the steps until the rhythm became familiar. Then he closed his eyes and let the small body sleep.

By four, he could read simple characters. Lady Seo taught him herself in the afternoons, sitting at the low table with a brush and a sheet of practice paper. She corrected his grip when it was too tight. She did not praise him when the characters came out clean; she simply nodded and set the next one. Eon matched her pace. He wrote the characters for mountain, for water, for fist, for quiet. He did not ask for harder ones. When she left the room to speak with a servant, he sometimes looked at the characters he had written and felt the old, distant amusement of someone who had once carved similar marks into far older tablets. Then he dipped the brush again and made the next stroke a little less steady than it could have been.

His father began to take him, once a month, to the outer training grounds to watch the senior disciples. They stood at the edge of the packed-earth yard while men and women in their twenties and thirties moved through forms that made the air hum. Baek Mun-gyeok did not explain the techniques. He simply watched, arms folded, and occasionally glanced down to see whether his son was paying attention. Eon watched. He noted the way one disciple’s shoulder rose too early on the third strike, the way another’s footwork left a small gap on the left side when she turned. He said nothing. When his father asked, afterward, what he had seen, Eon answered that the movements were fast and that some of the people looked very strong. His father accepted the answer. They walked back to the inner compound in silence.

The fifth year was the year the clan’s internal politics became visible even to a child who was trying not to look.

A cousin from the second branch, Baek Jin-ho, turned sixteen and was granted permission to attempt the clan’s intermediate examination. He passed. There was a small banquet. Eon was allowed to attend for the first hour. He sat between his mother and an elderly aunt and ate rice and grilled fish while the adults spoke in careful, layered sentences about Jin-ho’s future postings, about the Black Tiger Sect’s latest border probe, about the marriage prospects of several unwed daughters in the allied clans. No one asked Eon for his opinion. He was grateful.

Later that autumn, one of the outer disciples was expelled for stealing spirit herbs from the clan’s lower medicine garden. The story traveled through the servants’ quarters and reached the children’s practice yard in distorted form. Some of the older children claimed the thief had been trying to sell the herbs to a wandering merchant. Others said he had been caught using them to force a breakthrough he was not ready for. Eon listened, filed the details, and went back to practicing the fourth movement of the foundational form. His stance was still a little wide. Instructor So-yeon still corrected him. The monthly report still said ordinary.

Winter came early that year. Snow fell on the mountain for three days without stopping. The practice yards were cleared each morning, but the stone paths between buildings stayed slick. Eon walked them carefully, the way a child who had already fallen once would walk them. He did not fall again. In the evenings, Lady Seo sometimes called him to her room and let him sit near the brazier while she reviewed correspondence. She did not read the letters aloud. She simply worked, and he watched the way her brush moved, steady and precise, and the way she paused, once, over a particular line before continuing. He did not ask what the letter said. She did not offer.

On the morning of his fifth birthday, the clan held the small ceremony expected for a main-line child. There were offerings at the ancestor hall, a short speech from his father about duty and the continuation of the bloodline, and a modest meal. Eon received a new set of training clothes and a wooden practice sword of slightly better quality than the one he had been using. He bowed when he was supposed to bow. He thanked the elders when he was supposed to thank them. Afterward, in the quiet of his own room, he set the new sword on its stand and looked at it for a long time. The grain of the wood was even. The balance was acceptable for a child’s weapon. He picked it up, felt the weight, and put it down again without swinging it.

He was five. He could walk, speak, read simple texts, and perform the basic movements of his clan’s foundational art without embarrassing anyone. He had not broken any bones. He had not caused any scandals. He had not drawn the particular, hungry attention that sometimes fell on children who showed too much too soon. The elders spoke of him, when they spoke of him at all, as a quiet boy who would probably grow into a competent martial artist if he continued to apply himself. His mother watched him with the steady, measuring gaze of a woman who had already buried one child years before Eon was born and who did not intend to lose another to either illness or ambition. His father looked at him and saw, for the moment, a son who had not yet become either a problem or a solution.

Eon lay in his bed that night and listened to the wind move across the roof tiles. Somewhere in the compound, a senior disciple was still training; the sound of fists striking a wooden post carried faintly through the cold air. He counted the strikes until they stopped. Then he closed his eyes.

He had chosen not to stand out. So far, the choice had held.

Six months later, on a clear morning in late spring, his parents summoned him to the main reception hall.

The hall was not the grand audience chamber used for visiting sect leaders. It was the smaller room where the clan head received internal reports and settled family matters. The doors were open to the inner garden. Sunlight fell across the polished floor in long rectangles. Lady Seo sat to her husband’s right. Two elders of the main line sat to the left. A servant poured tea and withdrew.

Eon knelt where he was told to kneel. He kept his back straight and his hands on his knees. He did not fidget.

Baek Mun-gyeok spoke first.

“You are six years old next month,” he said. His voice was the same even tone he used when addressing junior disciples. “The clan has reached an agreement with the Han clan of the eastern river valleys. Their eldest daughter will be your fiancée.”

Eon blinked once. He had expected many things—additional training, a new instructor, perhaps a warning about the Black Tiger Sect’s movements—but not this. He kept his face still.

Lady Seo continued. “Her name is Han Seo-yeon. She is five years old. The Han clan holds the ferry crossings and the lower granaries along the Cheonggang. Their martial tradition emphasizes footwork and the use of the river mist for concealment. The marriage will be formalized when both of you reach sixteen, provided neither clan finds cause to break the agreement before then.”

One of the elders, an old man whose left hand was missing two fingers from an old duel, added, “The girl has black hair. Straight. She is said to be quiet. Her mother wrote that she prefers the garden to the practice yard, but that she completes her assigned forms without complaint. You will meet her once before the end of the year, at the autumn gathering. Until then, you will continue your current training. Do not speak of this arrangement to the other children.”

Eon bowed. “I understand.”

His father studied him for a moment. “Do you have questions?”

Eon considered. He could have asked about the Han clan’s current strength, about the exact terms of the alliance, about whether the girl had already begun internal energy training. Any of those questions would have been reasonable for a child who had been raised inside a major clan. They would also have marked him as someone who thought further ahead than most six-year-olds.

He shook his head. “No, Father.”

Baek Mun-gyeok nodded. The meeting ended.

Eon walked back to his room alone. The servant who usually accompanied him had been dismissed at the door of the hall. The corridors were empty at this hour; most of the household was either at morning training or at their assigned tasks. He passed the open doorway of the small library where the clan kept its genealogical records. He did not enter. He already knew, from overheard conversations and from the way the elders had spoken, roughly where the Han clan stood in the current balance of power. A marriage alliance with them would secure the river route and give the Azure Dragon Pavilion a quieter eastern border. It was practical. It was also, for a six-year-old, distant.

In his room, he sat at the low table and looked at the wooden sword on its stand. He thought about a girl with black hair whom he had not yet met. He thought about the way his mother had watched him while the elders spoke, and the way his father had not asked whether he was pleased or displeased. He thought about the years between six and sixteen, and about how many small, ordinary choices would have to be made in that time if he intended to remain unremarkable.

Outside, the mountain wind moved through the pines. Somewhere in the outer yard, Instructor So-yeon called the children’s class to order. Eon listened until the sound of their voices rose and fell in the familiar pattern of warm-up drills. Then he stood, straightened his clothes, and went to join them.

He arrived neither early nor late. He took his place in the middle of the line. When the instructor told them to assume the horse stance, he set his feet a little wider than perfect and held the position until his legs began to tremble at the expected moment. No one looked at him twice.

The engagement had been announced. The girl with black hair existed somewhere beyond the eastern ridges. For now, she was only a name and a future obligation. Eon breathed out, adjusted his stance by a fraction, and waited for the next count.

The autumn gathering was held at the neutral ground of the White Crane Rest Stop, three days’ ride east of Mount Cheongryong and two days west of the Han clan’s river holdings. It was not a grand assembly of sect leaders. It was the smaller, practical meeting the two clans held every three years to settle accounts on grain shipments, repair shared mountain paths, and confirm that neither side had quietly moved extra men toward the border ridges. This year it also served as the first formal introduction of the betrothed children.

Eon traveled with his mother, two elders, a physician, and a guard detail of twelve. He rode in a covered cart for most of the journey because Lady Seo did not trust a six-year-old’s balance on a mountain horse for three full days. He did not argue. Inside the cart he practiced the breathing pattern Instructor So-yeon had taught the children’s class the week before—slow inhale through the nose, hold for three heartbeats, controlled exhale—while the wheels jolted over roots and stones. By the second day the pattern no longer made his chest feel tight. He kept using it anyway.

The Rest Stop was a cluster of timber buildings around a wide stone courtyard that had once been a waystation for imperial couriers. Banners of both clans hung from the eaves, carefully spaced so neither flew higher than the other. Servants from both sides moved between the kitchens and the guest wings. Eon was given a small room on the Baek side of the compound. He changed into the formal indigo robe his mother had brought, combed his hair the way the servant showed him, and waited.

The meeting took place in the central hall at the hour of the goat. The adults sat on low platforms along the walls. The two children were brought forward and made to stand on the open floor between them, close enough to see each other clearly and far enough that neither could reach the other without taking several deliberate steps.

Han Seo-yeon was smaller than Eon by half a head. Her black hair was cut straight across her forehead and fell to the middle of her back, tied with a simple dark ribbon. She wore a pale gray-green robe with the Han clan’s river-wave pattern stitched in thread so fine it only showed when she moved. Her hands were clasped in front of her. She looked at Eon once, then lowered her eyes to the floorboards between them and kept them there.

An elder of the Han clan spoke the formal words of introduction. An elder of the Baek clan answered. The children were told to bow to each other. They did. Seo-yeon bowed a fraction deeper than necessary and held it a moment longer, the way children who have been drilled sometimes do when they are nervous. Eon matched the depth she had used and straightened at the same time she did.

Lady Seo asked, in a voice meant to be heard by the nearest adults but not by the entire hall, whether the children would like to walk in the small garden behind the hall while the elders finished their discussion of ferry tolls. Both children nodded. A servant from each clan followed at a distance.

The garden was narrow, bordered by a low wall and a line of carefully pruned plum trees that had already dropped most of their leaves. A stone path ran down the center. Seo-yeon walked on the left side of the path. Eon walked on the right. Neither of them spoke for the first twenty paces.

She broke the silence first, voice quiet.

“They said you train every morning.”

“Most mornings,” Eon answered. “The instructor makes us hold the horse stance until our legs shake. Then she makes us do it again.”

Seo-yeon glanced at him, then looked forward again. “Our instructor does the same. She says if your legs are still steady at the end, you were not standing correctly.”

“Ours says that too.”

They reached the end of the path, turned, and started back. A few yellow leaves drifted down between them. Seo-yeon watched one land on the stone and did not step on it.

“Do you like the sword or the fist better?” she asked.

Eon thought about the question. The truthful answer was that he liked neither more than the other at the moment, because both were still only tools he was deliberately using at half measure. The answer he gave was simpler.

“The fist. The sword is heavier. My wrists get tired.”

She nodded as if that made sense. “I like the short staff. It is lighter. But Mother says I have to learn the sword anyway because the clan expects it.”

They walked the length of the garden twice more. The servants stayed at the entrance and did not interrupt. When the time came to return to the hall, Seo-yeon stopped at the last plum tree and looked at him directly for the first time since the bow.

“They said we will not see each other again until the year we turn twelve,” she said. “Unless something happens with the border.”

“That is what they told me as well.”

She studied his face for a moment, then gave a small, careful nod. “All right.”

They went back inside. The adults finished their business by evening. The next morning the Baek party began the return journey. Eon rode in the cart again. He did not ask his mother what she thought of the Han girl. She did not offer an opinion. Three days later they were home, and the ordinary schedule resumed.

The years between six and nine were measured in training reports, seasonal examinations, and the small, private adjustments Eon made to how much of his growing strength he allowed anyone to see.

At seven he was moved from the children’s class into the junior disciples’ morning drills. The difference was immediate. The junior class trained for two full hours instead of one. They were expected to complete the entire foundational fist form without pausing to remember the next movement. Sparring was introduced—light contact, wooden weapons, and the strict rule that anyone who drew blood without permission would clean the practice yard for a month.

Eon lost his first three sparring matches.

He lost them cleanly, the way a boy who had only recently left the children’s class was expected to lose them. Against a nine-year-old cousin he was pushed out of the ring when his footwork lagged half a step behind. Against a retainer’s daughter he blocked two strikes correctly and then mistimed the third, taking a light tap to the shoulder that ended the exchange. Against an older boy from the third branch he simply ran out of breath before the count reached thirty and was told to sit down. Instructor Baek So-yeon, who now oversaw the junior class, wrote in her notes that the young master was adjusting to the increased pace and showed no unusual talent or unusual deficiency.

On the fourth match he did not lose.

The opponent was a boy named Kang Min-jae, eleven years old, solidly built, and already known for favoring heavy downward strikes. They circled. Min-jae stepped in and brought his wooden sword down in the same arc he had used to win his last four bouts. Eon raised his own sword to meet it. The impact should have driven his guard down and opened his ribs for the follow-up. Instead the wooden blades met, shuddered, and stopped. Min-jae’s eyes widened a fraction. He tried to disengage and reset. Eon’s sword was already moving, not fast, not flashy, just placed where Min-jae’s wrist had to travel if he wanted to recover his stance. The tip tapped the older boy’s forearm. The judge called the point.

They reset. Min-jae came in harder. Eon met the second strike the same way—solid, economical, a half-step inside the expected range—and tapped the shoulder on the return. Third exchange: Min-jae feinted high and cut low. Eon had already shifted his weight. The low cut passed through empty air. His riposte caught Min-jae across the upper arm before the older boy could recover. Three points. Match ended.

Min-jae stared at him. The other juniors had gone quiet. So-yeon studied Eon for a long moment, then told both boys to bow and return to the line. Later, in her monthly report, she wrote that the young master had shown improved timing against a heavier opponent and recommended continued observation. She did not write that he had won. She wrote that the match had been decided by small errors in the opponent’s recovery. The distinction mattered. No one began calling Eon a prodigy. A few of the older juniors began choosing different partners when the instructor allowed free sparring. Eon continued to lose, carefully, to anyone who was not actively trying to injure him.

His body changed faster than the reports suggested.

At eight his shoulders had broadened enough that the training robes from the previous year pulled across the back. The clan physician checked his meridians during the spring examination and noted that the flow through the lung and heart channels was “unusually even for his age.” The physician did not say the flow was strong. He said it was even. Lady Seo, who read every examination note, asked Eon that evening whether he had been practicing any breathing methods beyond what the instructors taught. He told her he sometimes counted his breaths while he waited for sleep. She accepted the answer and did not ask again.

He began waking an hour before the morning bell. The junior dormitory was still dark. He sat on the edge of his pallet, spine straight, and moved a thin thread of internal energy through the major channels—up the spine, down the front, once through each limb—then let it settle. The amount was small enough that a casual sweep by any elder would register only as the ordinary background qi of a healthy child. He did this every morning for six months. By the end of the sixth month the thread moved without conscious effort. His resting pulse had slowed by four beats. He still finished mid-pack in the endurance runs.

At nine the clan held the annual junior examination. It consisted of three parts: recitation of the clan precepts, performance of the foundational form in front of two elders, and a single sparring match against a randomly selected opponent from the same age group. Eon recited the precepts without error and without flourish. His form was correct in every required position and half a finger off in two of the transitions the elders watched most closely. The sparring match was against a girl from the second branch who had won her last five bouts by relentless pressure. She opened with a fast series of jabs. Eon gave ground, blocked three, slipped the fourth, and tapped her wrist on the counter. She reset and came again. He yielded the center of the ring, forced her to overextend on the sixth exchange, and ended the match with a light touch to the side of her ribs. The judge called it. The girl bowed, breathing hard. Eon bowed back, also breathing hard. One of the elders made a note. The other simply nodded and moved to the next pair.

“The river route,” Eon said. “And the eastern border.”

His father studied him. “The Han clan sent a letter last month. Their daughter continues her training. She has begun learning the mist-step footwork earlier than most. They asked whether we would permit a short visit next spring—three days, here at the Pavilion—so the children can train in the same yard under supervision. I have not yet answered.”

Eon kept his hands still on his knees. “What does Mother think?”

“She thinks three days is short enough that neither child will form inconvenient habits, and long enough that we will see whether they can stand in the same space without creating trouble for the instructors.” Baek Mun-gyeok paused. “Do you object?”

“No, Father.”

The letter was sent the following week. The Han clan accepted. The visit was set for the third month of the following year, when both children would be ten.

In the months between the examination and the visit, Eon continued the pattern he had already set. He trained with the juniors. He lost matches he could have won and won matches that would have looked suspicious if he had lost them. He woke early, circulated a thin thread of qi, and went back to sleep for the final half hour before the bell. His strength increased. The increase showed in small ways: he could hold the horse stance for the full count without his legs trembling at the end; he could complete the endurance course without the sharp stitch in his side that still slowed several of his age-mates; when he blocked a heavier opponent’s strike, the impact no longer traveled all the way up his arm and into his shoulder. None of these changes were large enough, on any single day, to make an instructor rewrite a report.

The instructors noticed anyway.

So-yeon began pairing him, more often than chance would suggest, with partners who fought dirty or who relied on raw power. She never explained the pairings. Eon never asked. He met each one, gave ground when giving ground was reasonable, and ended the exchanges before anyone was injured. After one particularly rough session against a thirteen-year-old who liked to hook ankles, So-yeon kept him back while the others left.

“You are choosing when to win,” she said. It was not a question.

Eon looked at the packed earth between them. “I do not want to clean the yard for a month.”

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, “If you continue to choose that carefully, some of the branch-family boys will eventually decide you are insulting them. When that happens, they will not fight you in the ring. They will find you between classes. I cannot watch every path on the mountain.”

“I understand, Instructor.”

“See that you do.” She dismissed him.

He did not change the pattern. He only added one more calculation: which of the older boys were proud enough to take a public loss as a slight, and which were practical enough to treat it as information. He lost to the proud ones when the loss could be blamed on footing or fatigue. He beat the practical ones cleanly enough that they stopped seeking him out. The distinction held through the winter.

Eon was in the junior yard finishing the last set of the foundational form when the bell at the main gate rang twice—the signal for expected guests. He completed the form, bowed to the instructor, and went to wash his face and change into a clean robe. By the time he reached the inner courtyard, the Han riders were already dismounting.

Han Seo-yeon stepped down from her horse without assistance. She had grown. The top of her head now reached Eon’s chin. Her black hair was longer, still straight, tied higher than it had been at the Rest Stop so that it would not interfere with training. She wore travel clothes rather than formal robes: dark gray, practical, with the Han wave pattern only on the cuffs. She saw him across the courtyard, inclined her head the precise degree required between betrothed children of allied clans, and turned to speak with the Han elder who had accompanied her.

Lady Seo greeted the guests. Rooms were assigned. The evening meal was formal and short. The real purpose of the visit would begin at dawn the next day, when both children were scheduled to join the same junior class under So-yeon’s supervision.

Eon returned to his room after the meal. He sat on the edge of the pallet and ran through the morning’s breathing pattern until his pulse settled. Somewhere in the guest wing, a door closed. Footsteps moved along the outer veranda and stopped. He did not rise to look. The footsteps moved on again after a moment.

He lay down, hands folded on his stomach, and listened to the mountain. The wind was lighter than it had been in winter. In the junior yard the wooden posts had been repaired; the new timber still smelled faintly of resin. Tomorrow he would stand in the same line as the girl with black hair, and he would have to decide, in front of instructors from both clans, exactly how much of what he could do was safe to show.

He closed his eyes. The decision could wait until the first stance was called.
